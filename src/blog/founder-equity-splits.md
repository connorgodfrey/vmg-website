# How to Split Founder Equity When "Equal" Isn't Obvious

<img src="/assets/founder-equity-splits.jpg" alt="Four co-founders watch a scale tip: time invested, skills, and risk taken outweigh a single equity-share block — splitting equity by real contribution" class="blog-hero-img" loading="lazy">

Two founders agree to split a company evenly, shake on it, and never write it down. They assume that settles the question. Under New York law it does not, and the arrangement they end up with may not be the one they think they have.

An equal split is often the right answer. But it has to be a decision recorded in an operating agreement rather than an assumption, for two reasons: the statute fills the gap on terms most founders do not expect, and where contributions are genuinely unequal, an even split defers the disagreement instead of settling it.

---

## What New York Does If You Never Decide

Form a New York LLC without addressing the split and the statute does not assume 50/50. Absent a provision in the operating agreement, profits, losses, and distributions are allocated **in proportion to the value of each member's contributions** as stated in the company's records (LLC Law §§ 503, 504), and voting power follows the profit share (§ 402). Two founders who shook hands as "equal partners" while one contributed $40,000 and the other $5,000 do not have an equal partnership. They have a company whose default terms point somewhere else, and no written record of what they meant to agree to.

General partnerships run the opposite way: partners share profits equally regardless of what each contributed (Partnership Law § 40). Same handshake, opposite result, turning on a formation question the founders may not remember deciding.

The practical point holds in both directions. If you do not write the split down, the statute writes it for you, and it has no way of knowing what you intended.

## Start With What Each Founder Actually Brings

Before choosing a framework, inventory what each founder is contributing:

- **Cash** contributed, and cash *forgone* (the founder working full-time without market salary is contributing that salary).
- **Work product brought in**: the codebase, the curriculum, the client methodology, the brand.
- **Time commitment**: full-time versus nights-and-weekends is the most common asymmetry, and the most commonly papered over.
- **Distribution**: the relationships and reputation that will produce the first twenty customers.
- **Operating burden**: who actually runs the company day to day.
- **Replaceability**: what would it cost to hire this founder's contribution at market rates?

One weighting principle: equity mostly pays for the future, not the past. A head start matters, but four years of full-time execution usually matters more.

## Three Ways to Get to a Number

**1. Contribution-weighted scoring.** List the categories above, weight them for your business (distribution might be worth double in a sales-led company), score each founder candidly, and translate to percentages. The output is not scientific and does not need to be. Its value is the discussion it forces: every gap between a founder's self-assessment and the scorecard is a conversation worth having before the company has value.

**2. A dynamic split.** Rather than fixing percentages on day one, ownership accrues based on logged contributions (hours, cash, deliverables) until a defined freeze event such as a financing or a revenue threshold. This approach, popularized as "Slicing Pie," is the most honest where the future is genuinely uncertain and the most administratively demanding, since it only works if the logging actually happens.

**3. Equal control, unequal economics.** The least understood of the three, and the one that makes the best use of the LLC form. An operating agreement can separate the three things a single percentage usually bundles: votes, profits, and capital. You can [hold governance at 50/50](/blog/fifty-fifty-deadlock/), equal partners on every decision, while the economics track contribution. The cash founder's contribution can return first through a distribution preference or be structured as a loan. The full-time founder can take a guaranteed payment, the LLC equivalent of a salary, before profits are split. Residual profits can divide on any ratio you choose, and can shift over time. Where a standoff over percentages is really about control and recognition rather than money, separating the two makes the economics considerably easier to settle.

## Vesting as the Fallback

When founders cannot agree whether the split should be 50/50 or 60/40, one workable answer is to grant equal and let both interests vest. A founder who leaves in month ten self-corrects the split through the vesting schedule, and the founder who stays four years earns the equality in fact. (How LLC vesting works, including the 30-day tax election, is covered in [this series' companion memo on founder vesting](/blog/llc-founder-vesting/).)

## Equity Moves That Age Badly

A few allocations reliably cause problems later: granting meaningful equity for a name on an advisory slide; paying for one-time early work, a logo or an introduction or a build sprint, with permanent equity instead of cash or a contractor agreement; and deferring the split on the theory that it will be clearer later. Repricing ownership once the company has value is substantially harder than setting it at formation, and it can carry tax consequences that a day-one allocation would not.

---

## Conclusion

There is nothing wrong with 50/50 where it reflects the contributions. Two founders, both full-time, comparable inputs, is a common and defensible arrangement. The problem is 50/50 chosen to avoid the conversation and never written down, which leaves the statute to supply an answer neither founder picked. Run the inventory, choose a framework, and use the dials the LLC gives you: control, cash-out priority, draws, and vesting can each carry part of the load. The test is whether both founders can still explain the split, out loud, in year three.
