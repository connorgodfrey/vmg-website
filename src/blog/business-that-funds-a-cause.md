# How Should You Structure a Business That Funds a Cause?

<img src="/assets/business-that-funds-a-cause.jpg" alt="Blueprint on a drafting table showing a for-profit shop and a nonprofit joined by a bridge, a benefit corporation, and the outline of a single combined entity" class="blog-hero-img" loading="lazy">

If your goal is to sell products and send the profits to a cause, you usually need only a for-profit company. The company can deduct part of what it gives, and you keep control. You need a nonprofit when the cause has to raise money from other people: donors can deduct gifts only to a 501(c)(3) charity, and most foundation grants and many government grants are limited to tax-exempt organizations. In that case the selling should usually happen in a separate for-profit entity. A single nonprofit that sells products works when the selling itself serves the mission or when volunteers do nearly all the work. Benefit corporations and B Corp certification are a fourth path: they write a mission into a for-profit's charter and hold it to public reporting, but they do not change its taxes or let it take deductible donations.

The rest of this memo explains each option, what it costs, and where founders get into trouble.

---

## Option 1: A For-Profit Company That Gives

The simplest structure is an ordinary LLC or corporation that sells products and donates some or all of its profits to charities it chooses. There is no IRS exemption application, no registration or annual filing with the Attorney General's Charities Bureau, and no legal requirement to recruit an outside board, though outside advisors are still worth having. You keep full control, and you can change the cause, the percentage, or the plan whenever you want.

The tradeoff is tax. The company's profits are taxed before they reach the charity, and the deduction for giving them away is limited:

- **LLCs taxed as partnerships or sole proprietorships** pass the donation through to the owners, who deduct it on their personal returns. Beginning in 2026, owners who itemize can deduct only gifts above 0.5% of their adjusted gross income, and cash gifts to public charities remain capped at 60% of AGI. Owners who take the standard deduction get a new deduction of up to $1,000 ($2,000 joint) for cash gifts to public charities, which is small relative to the profits of a working business.
- **C corporations** deduct gifts only above 1% and up to 10% of taxable income, starting in 2026.

So a for-profit that gives away "100% of profits" is usually paying tax on part of those profits first. For a small business the dollar difference may be modest. For a large one it is real.

There is a partial exception. A payment to a charity that bears a direct relationship to the business and is made with a reasonable expectation of a commensurate financial return, such as a sponsorship or a promotion designed to drive sales, may be deductible as an ordinary business expense under Treas. Reg. § 1.162-15(a) instead of as a charitable gift, and the floors and caps above do not apply to it. Whether a particular giving program qualifies depends on the facts, so confirm the treatment with a tax advisor before you build pricing or marketing around it.

The other constraint is what you tell customers. A for-profit cannot call itself a nonprofit, and a customer's purchase is not a tax-deductible donation no matter where the money goes. If you advertise that purchases benefit a named charity, New York treats you as a commercial co-venturer under Executive Law § 173-a. That requires a written contract with the charity before the promotion starts and an accounting to the charity within 90 days after it ends (at least annually for ongoing promotions), showing items sold and amounts paid. The charity then reports those arrangements to the Attorney General. A willful violation is a misdemeanor. These rules apply even when you control both the business and the charity.

## Option 2: A Nonprofit That Sells

A nonprofit can sell products. Museum shops, thrift stores, and bakeries run by charities do it every day. The question is whether the sales count as part of the charity's mission or as an unrelated business.

Selling candles to fund an animal rescue is not related to the mission simply because the money goes there. The IRS looks at the activity, not where the income ends up. Income from a trade or business that is regularly carried on and not substantially related to the exempt purpose is unrelated business taxable income, taxed at the 21% corporate rate and reported on Form 990-T once gross unrelated income reaches $1,000. If unrelated activity grows large enough to become a primary purpose of the organization, the exemption itself is at risk.

Three situations change that answer:

- **The selling is the mission.** Goodwill and Homeboy Industries sell goods and food, but the businesses exist to train and employ the people they serve. When the work itself carries out the charitable purpose, the income is related.
- **Volunteers do substantially all the work.** Under IRC § 513(a)(1), a business is not unrelated if substantially all the work is performed without compensation. There is no bright-line percentage; courts have applied the exception where about 91% of the labor was unpaid and refused it where about 23% was paid. A volunteer-run bake sale or holiday market usually qualifies. A business with a paid production staff does not.
- **The goods were donated.** Selling merchandise that was contributed to the organization is excluded under § 513(a)(3), which is why thrift stores work.

Running the business inside the nonprofit also means the founder cannot own it. A 501(c)(3) has no owners, its assets are permanently dedicated to charitable purposes, and pay to insiders must be reasonable. A founder who later wants to sell the business, take profits, or bring in investors will find the nonprofit is the wrong vehicle.

Forming the nonprofit itself in New York costs a $75 filing fee for the certificate of incorporation under the Not-for-Profit Corporation Law. Some purposes, including many health and mental health services, require a state agency's consent before the Department of State will accept the filing. Federal exemption requires Form 1023 or, for organizations projecting under $50,000 in annual gross receipts and under $250,000 in assets, the streamlined Form 1023-EZ ($275). Before soliciting donations in New York, most charities must register with the Attorney General's Charities Bureau and then file annual reports.

## Option 3: Two Entities

When the founder wants both a real business and a real charity, the usual answer is two entities: a for-profit LLC that sells, and a nonprofit that receives donations, grants, and some or all of the LLC's profits. This is the structure most small founders end up with, and it is where the details matter most.

**Sister entities under common control.** The founder owns the LLC and sits on the nonprofit's board. That overlap is permitted, but every transaction between the two is a related party transaction under N-PCL § 715. Before the nonprofit enters one, its board must determine that the terms are fair, reasonable, and in the nonprofit's best interest; the interested director cannot take part in that decision; and where the founder has a substantial financial interest, the board must consider alternatives and document its reasoning. New York charities must also adopt a conflict of interest policy. On the federal side, a 501(c)(3) that overpays an insider (above-market rent, a sweetheart supply contract, a royalty to the founder's LLC) exposes the insider to excise taxes on the excess benefit and the charity to scrutiny of its exemption.

The practical rules follow from that: separate bank accounts and books, written agreements for anything shared (office space, staff, the brand name), arm's-length pricing, and a nonprofit board with directors who are not the founder's employees or family.

**A nonprofit that owns the business.** The parent-subsidiary version flips ownership: the nonprofit owns the for-profit outright. Dividends from the subsidiary to the nonprofit are generally not unrelated business income. Interest, rent, and royalties paid by a subsidiary that the nonprofit controls (more than 50% ownership) generally are, to the extent they reduce the subsidiary's taxable income. That rule catches founders who try to move profits up as "license fees" rather than dividends.

Two well-known examples use this model. Newman's Own Foundation is the sole owner of Newman's Own, Inc., and reports giving more than $600 million since 1982. Private foundations generally cannot own a controlling stake in a business, so Congress added an exception in the Philanthropic Enterprise Act of 2018 for foundation-owned companies that meet independence and payout requirements. The Mozilla Foundation created the Mozilla Corporation in 2005 as a wholly owned taxable subsidiary to receive Firefox search revenue, much of it from Google, after recognizing that revenue did not fit comfortably inside a charity. The IRS still audited the Foundation's search royalties, and the audit closed in 2012 with a $1.5 million settlement. Structure reduces risk; it does not eliminate it.

## Mission-Locked For-Profits: Benefit Corporations and B Corps

Founders often say "B corp" to mean two different things. One is a legal entity type. The other is a private certification. A company can have either, both, or neither.

**Benefit corporations** are a statutory form. New York has authorized them since 2012 under Article 17 of the Business Corporation Law. A New York benefit corporation must have a purpose of creating a general public benefit, its directors must consider the effects of their decisions on employees, customers, the community, and the environment as well as shareholders, and it must deliver an annual benefit report to shareholders within 120 days after year end. The report measures performance against a third-party standard, is filed with the Department of State, and must be posted on the company's website if it has one. An existing corporation can convert by amending its certificate of incorporation with a supermajority shareholder vote.

Delaware offers the public benefit corporation under DGCL §§ 361 to 368, and since 2018 a statutory public benefit LLC. New York has no benefit LLC. Watch the vocabulary: in New York, a "public benefit corporation" is a state authority such as the MTA, not a private company. The low-profit LLC, or L3C, a hybrid some states created to attract foundation investment, is not available in New York and has seen little use; at least one state has repealed it.

None of these forms is tax-exempt. A benefit corporation is taxed like any other corporation, customers' purchases are not deductible, and it cannot accept tax-deductible donations.

What the form adds is mostly obligation: the directors must weigh stakeholders in their decisions, and the company must publish an annual report that measures it against an outside standard. What it gives back is narrower than founders expect. Directors of an ordinary corporation already have wide latitude. Courts defer to business decisions that have a rational business purpose, and BCL § 717(b) expressly allows New York directors to consider employees, customers, and the community. A founder who controls an ordinary corporation can run it for a mission without changing its form.

The legal difference shows up in three places. First, when the company is for sale or outside investors are pressing for returns, a benefit corporation's directors must weigh the mission and are protected when they do. In New York that turns a permission into a duty. In Delaware the gap is wider, because the directors of an ordinary corporation that is being sold are expected to get the best price for the shareholders. Second, the mission is harder to undo: in New York, dropping benefit status or selling substantially all of the assets takes a three-quarters shareholder vote. Third, the status is a signal to customers, employees, and mission-aligned investors. For a company with one owner and no plans to sell or raise outside money, the signal is most of what the form buys, and the reporting is a real cost to weigh against it.

**B Corp certification** comes from B Lab, a nonprofit, not the state. B Lab revised its standards: companies seeking first-time certification in 2026 are assessed against topic-based requirements across seven impact areas (including governance, fair work, human rights, and climate action) rather than the former 80-point score, and existing B Corps move to the new standards as they recertify. Certification carries annual fees and requires stakeholder considerations in the company's governing documents, which for a corporation in a state like New York typically means becoming a benefit corporation.

Warby Parker is a Delaware public benefit corporation and a certified B Corp built around a "Buy a Pair, Give a Pair" program; it went public in 2021 with that structure intact. The form is not what keeps the program alive. An ordinary corporation could run the same program as a business decision, and the co-founders held about 48% of the voting power at the listing through shares that carry ten votes each. What the public benefit charter adds is notice to public investors that the board will weigh the mission against the share price, and legal cover for the board when it does, which would matter most if the company were ever for sale.

Ben & Jerry's shows the limits of protecting a mission by contract. It was a public company whose shareholders were bought out for cash when Unilever acquired it in 2000, so the mission could be protected only by agreement with the new owner. The merger agreement gave an independent board primary responsibility for the social mission and the integrity of the brand, and left financial and operational control with Unilever. Years of public disputes followed, first with Unilever and then with The Magnum Ice Cream Company, which took over the brand when Unilever spun off its ice cream business in 2025. By January 2026, six of the board's eight directors had left or been removed. In August 2026 a federal judge dismissed most of the directors' lawsuit, holding that the agreement let them enforce only their own appointment and removal rights. The right to enforce the mission provisions belonged to the company itself, a subsidiary the parent owns. Their challenge to the removals is still pending. The agreement could have given the independent directors an express right to enforce every mission provision, and the means to pay for it. It did not, and a mission right that only the owner can enforce protects very little.

Patagonia is the most cited example and the least typical. It is a California benefit corporation and a certified B Corp. In 2022, founder Yvon Chouinard and his family transferred all of the company's voting stock (about 2% of the equity) to the Patagonia Purpose Trust, which the family oversees, and all of the nonvoting stock (about 98%) to the Holdfast Collective, a 501(c)(4) social welfare organization. Patagonia said profits not reinvested in the business, roughly $100 million a year, would go to Holdfast for climate and conservation work. The family reportedly paid about $17.5 million in gift tax on the trust transfer and took no charitable deduction, because gifts to a 501(c)(4) are not deductible. The family chose a (c)(4) over a (c)(3) because a (c)(4) can lobby without limit and engage in some political campaign activity, so long as that is not its primary activity, and the trust kept voting control with the family. That combination of mission, control, and political flexibility cost millions in planning and tax. The lesson for a small founder is not to copy the structure but to start with the same questions: who controls the business, where the money goes, and what the cause needs to do with it.

## Before Forming a Nonprofit: Fiscal Sponsorship

A founder who is not sure the charitable side will last can test it under a fiscal sponsor: an established 501(c)(3) that accepts tax-deductible donations for the project, holds the funds, and charges an administrative fee, typically a percentage of funds raised. The sponsor has legal control over the money, so the agreement matters. If the project grows, it can spin out into its own nonprofit later. This avoids a year of exemption paperwork for an idea that may change shape.

## How to Choose

Four questions usually settle it:

- **Does the cause need to raise money from other people?** If the business will be its only funder, a for-profit that gives is simpler and keeps you in control. If you want donors to get a tax deduction, or you want to apply for grants limited to charities, you need a nonprofit or a fiscal sponsor.
- **Who does the work, and is the work itself the mission?** Volunteer labor or mission-driven employment can support a single nonprofit. A business with paid staff selling to the public belongs in a for-profit.
- **Do you want to own the business?** If you may want to sell it, draw income from it, or raise investment, it cannot live inside a charity.
- **How durable should the mission be?** A donation pledge can be changed tomorrow. A benefit corporation, a charity-owned subsidiary, or a Patagonia-style trust each lock the mission in more firmly, at increasing cost.

## Conclusion

A business with a mission can be one entity or two, but the choice should follow from what the cause needs. A for-profit that gives is the default for most small founders. A nonprofit that sells works only within the unrelated business rules. Two entities give you a real business and a real charity, at the price of governance discipline between them. Benefit corporation status and B Corp certification commit a for-profit to its mission without changing its taxes.
